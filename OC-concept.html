OC Opportunity Campus85 Jamaica Avenue, Plainview
85 Jamaica Avenue, Plainview, NY 11803

In January 2025 the District asked for $14.4 million to fix Jamaica Avenue. Voters said no, more than two to one.

That money would have turned 85 Jamaica Avenue into instructional space for students, at a time when enrollment is rising and JFK High School and Judy Jacobs Parkway Elementary are crowded. The proposition failed 1,244 to 2,585.1 The building still needs the work, and the District still needs the space.

Opportunity Campus is one idea for paying for that renovation with private money instead of a tax increase, giving the District classroom space back at no capital cost, and filling the rest of the building with childcare, sports, career discovery and community programs that pay their own way.

It has not been proposed to the District. Nothing has been decided. Before it goes further we want to know whether Plainview families would use it, what it should cost, and what would have to be true for you to support it.

Doing nothing has a price too. The building keeps aging, the District keeps paying to hold it, and the space the District said it needed stays unusable for students.

The idea

Someone else pays for the renovation. The District keeps the land and gets classrooms back.

The premise is simple enough to state in four lines, and every line still has to survive testing.

How it would work

  1. Private capital funds the building work. No bond, no borrowing, no increase to the tax levy.
  2. The District keeps ownership of the land for the entire term, with the right to take the building back on defined triggers.
  3. A defined block of instructional space goes back to the District at no capital cost, addressing part of what Proposition 2 was meant to address.
  4. The rest of the building carries community uses that pay for themselves: childcare, indoor sports, career discovery, enrichment and event space, run by operators under terms the District approves.

The honest counterargument: private money is never free. A capital partner earns a return, and that return comes from program fees, ground rent or both. Whether the arithmetic works at a price Plainview families would actually pay is the open question, and it is what the survey is for.

Instructional space for the District

Classrooms and program space returned to the District under the lease, at no capital cost to taxpayers. This is why the District cared about the building in the first place.

Childcare and early learning

Licensed, full-day, dependable. Families already rely on childcare at Jamaica Avenue, and any path forward has to protect that first.

Sports and recreation

Indoor courts and turf for practice, leagues and tournaments. A reason families use the campus weekly rather than once a year.

Career discovery

Kids get to try real tools, real technology and real industries years before anyone asks them to pick a path.

Learning and enrichment

Camps, classes, maker space and school-break programs, in rooms flexible enough to change as demand changes.

The operative number is the swing: 33% to 80%.

Capital work requiring a bond drew roughly one third support. Capital work carrying no tax impact drew roughly four fifths. The work did not change. The payer did. Our reading is that this community will fund improvements to its buildings and will not fund them through the levy. If that reading is right, the constraint on Jamaica Avenue is a source of capital rather than a lack of will.

The counterargument a board member should press. Proposition 2 failed by a wider margin than the far larger proposition on the same ballot. That is consistent with our reading, and it is equally consistent with residents specifically objecting to investment in that building. We do not know which, and the community survey now asks directly. Until it is answered, treat this as an inference rather than a finding.

04

The first objection: why give up a building you told voters you needed?

It is the right question and it deserves a direct answer rather than a reframe. Enrollment has risen from 4,972 to 5,629 over four years,3 and the District has publicly described crowding at JFK High School and Judy Jacobs Parkway Elementary.1 A structure that takes instructional capacity away from a growing district would be a bad deal, and the Board should reject any version of this that does.

Instructional space comes off the top, not out of the leftovers.

Before any commercial use is programmed, a defined block of the building is reserved for the District, built to District specification, delivered at no capital cost and held under the lease for the full term. The private uses occupy and pay for the balance. In effect the District receives part of what Proposition 2 would have bought, funded by someone else, and gives up the portion of the building it was not going to be able to renovate anyway.

The size of that block is the central commercial term of this concept, and we will not name a number before diligence supports one. A larger District block means less revenue-generating area, a weaker return, less capital available, and less of the identified work funded. That curve is real and we will bring it to the Board quantified rather than described.

ScenarioWhat the District ends up with
Status quoThe building as it is, $14.4 million of identified work unfunded, annual carrying cost continuing, no additional instructional capacity.
Another bond attemptPossible full control and full instructional use of the building, funded by the levy, subject to a vote that drew 33 percent support eighteen months ago.
This structureA defined block of instructional space at no capital cost, the building renovated without a levy increase, ground rent, fee title retained, and less than the whole building available for District use.

Row two is a legitimate choice and the Board may prefer it. A third bond attempt is foreclosed by nothing here, and this concept should be judged against that option rather than against doing nothing.

06

Questions for counsel, which we are not going to answer ourselves.

New York places specific requirements on how a school district may lease or dispose of property, and we are not the right party to interpret them. We are working through these with [[COUNSEL_FIRM]] and will publish what we learn. The District's own counsel should answer them independently. Nothing in these materials assumes any particular structure is permitted.

  1. Under what authority may the District lease this property, and for what maximum term?
  2. Does a lease of the contemplated length require voter approval, and if so in what form?If yes, this concept faces the same electorate that rejected Proposition 2, and the case would rest on the absence of a tax impact.
  3. Must the property first be declared no longer needed for school purposes, and what does that determination trigger?Complicated here, because the District has publicly stated that it does need the space.
  4. Is a competitive procurement required, in what form, and does an unsolicited proposal foreclose it?
  5. Do deed restrictions, reverters or original acquisition conditions attach to this parcel?
  6. What is the property tax treatment of a private leasehold on District-owned land, and would a PILOT be required or available?
  7. SEQRA: who is lead agency, what classification applies, and what level of review follows?
  8. Is the property subject to Town of Oyster Bay zoning and site plan review, and does the contemplated use conform?
  9. Does prevailing wage apply to construction on District-owned land under a private lease?
  10. What are the existing childcare agreements, and what are their terms on assignment, relocation and termination?
  11. What disclosure and conflict rules apply to a private party engaging the District on a property proposal?

If the answer to question one or question two is unfavorable, this concept ends there, and it should. We would rather find that out in month one than month twelve.

09

Control and reversion, which is what a board should press hardest on.

The fair objection to any long-term arrangement is that a future board inherits it and cannot undo it. These are the protections we would expect the District to demand, and we would rather propose them than be asked for them.

  1. Fee title stays with the District for the entire term.No sale, no option to purchase, no right of first refusal on the land.
  2. Permitted-use schedule with District approval over changes.Uses are enumerated in the lease. Adding or substituting one requires District consent, so the campus cannot drift into something the community rejected.
  3. Reversion triggers on default, abandonment and performance failure.Defined, measurable and enforceable without litigation where possible. Extended vacancy should be a trigger.
  4. Step-in and replacement-operator rights.If an operator fails, the District has a defined path to a replacement rather than an empty building it cannot control.
  5. District priority access.Guaranteed hours and terms for District athletics, programs and community use, defined in the lease rather than promised in a presentation.
  6. Return-condition standard with security.The asset comes back in a defined condition, verified by inspection, with funds held against the standard.
  7. Design and site approval by the District.Height, massing, lighting, hours and buffers approved before construction and enforceable afterward.
  8. Audit and reporting rights.Annual reporting on use, programming, community access and reserve funding, delivered in public session.
  9. Assignment restrictions.The District approves any transfer of the leasehold. The counterparty three years from now should be one the District chose.

The honest tradeoff

Every protection above has a cost. Tighter control raises the return a capital partner requires, which lowers ground rent and shrinks the share of the identified work that private money can absorb. A board should expect that tradeoff quantified rather than described. We will bring the numbers when we have them.

  • New York districts that leased underused buildings to private or nonprofit operators, and how those arrangements are performing now
  • Cases where a district leased property and later needed it back, and what reversion actually cost

11

What we do not know yet.

A board can only trust the strong claims in a document if the weak ones are labeled. These are the open items that could end the concept.

  • Why voters actually rejected Proposition 2. Our reading is the tax impact. It may have been the building. The community survey asks directly, and if the answer is the building, this concept has a problem no financing structure fixes.
  • Whether demand exists at a price that works. The survey asks what households would pay. Until responses come in, the revenue side is an assumption.
  • How much instructional space the District actually needs here, and whether a partial block is worth anything to it.
  • Whether the site can absorb the traffic. No traffic study has been done. If the answer is no, the concept shrinks or ends.
  • What the work costs today. The $14.4 million figure is a 2025 estimate that needs escalating, and we have not seen the Building Condition Survey behind it.
  • Whether the legal path exists. See section 06. Two of those eleven questions can end this on their own.
  • Whether operators will commit. Interest is not a lease. No operator has signed anything, and none has been asked to.
  • Whether capital will price it. No financing has been arranged and no terms have been quoted.
  • Environmental condition. No Phase I has been performed on a building of this era.
  • Whether the community wants it. That is the point of the community view of this site, and the answer may be no.
